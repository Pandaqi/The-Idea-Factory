## Young Adult vs Adult

**What makes a book (not) YA?** <https://www.goodreads.com/topic/show/588075-what-makes-a-book-not-ya>

**Lesson one:** YA is not a genre, it's an *age group* or, more generally, an *audience*. It's about who will read your book and to whom its primarily targeted.

Additionally, these days, it's a marketing term. Publishers think a book will sell better with a YA label? They'll label it that way.

**Lesson two:** age determines *growth* and *introspection*

-   Very young protagonists are just there for the ride: they just want an adventure, they do stuff, they don't necessarily grow or look at their actions.
-   YA protagonists have introspection but lack any experience. It's a lot of doubts, split-second decisions, and a lot of "firsts"
-   Adult protagonists are the same way but have much more experience and backstory.

**Lesson three**: it's about *tone* and *presentation*.

-   A children's book has a light tone, adventurous, comical.
-   A YA novel must have a clear tone that immediately conveys the thoughts, personality, lifestyle, etc. of the teenage protagonists.
-   An adult novel doesn't need such a clear tone and can be anything.

Additionally, any story can be presented through different lenses. When writing a YA novel, make sure to write the story *from the perspective* of the teenage characters as much as possible. (There can be older and younger protagonists, but we don't see things from *their* perspective that often.)

**Lesson four:** it's about the *themes* being explored. Typical YA overall themes are "coming of age" and "discovery of oneself", especially looking at (sexual) maturity.

YA is about *teaching* (the characters don't know stuff yet, have no experience, morals and perspectives aren't set in stone or fully developed), whilst adult literature is about *reflection* (looking back at the past, at decisions, at relationships, looking at your life through a different perspective).

More specific themes are:

-   Love and romance
-   Sense of responsibility
-   Holding up to your values
-   Facing your enemies
-   Being kind and Polite
-   Angst / insecurity
-   Where do I belong?
-   More??

**Lesson five:** there are many misconceptions. People think YA cannot have explicit language, or sex scenes, or be complex or dark. This simply isn't true. The only thing to realize is that using these elements narrows your audience and your change of being published.

**Lesson six:** the only real, consistent difference is that YA has teenage protagonists (and is mostly written from their point of view) and a much faster writing flow (faster pace, simpler vocabulary, stating things directly instead of alluding to it). Often this *does* result in a loss of nuance and subtlety.

**Lesson seven:** perhaps look at it in a different way. YA is ...

-   The wonder of looking at the world through inexperienced eyes
-   The difficult but beautiful transition from childhood to adulthood
-   Doing and experiencing things for the first time
-   Failing, experimenting, falling a lot ... and then standing up and growing.

## APPENDIX A: A very good summary of all book elements:

-   **Pacing?** Get to the action and keep us going
-   **Emotions?** Feelings are a plus here
-   **Hope?** You can be sad/dark, just give us a future
-   **Setting?** Recognizable to the teen
-   **Questions it answers?** "Who am I, who do I love, what do I want, who do I want to be, and what do I know to be true?"

A good (main) character in a YA novel, is one who could **not** be any other age. If they were younger or older, the story, setting and themes would not fit at all.
