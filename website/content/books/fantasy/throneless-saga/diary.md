## What's the idea?
### Once there were (board) games
Once upon a time, I thought about creating little games one could play while _waiting_. For example, when you're queuing for a rollercoaster in a theme park, you could just play a fun game (with cards, for example) to pass the time.

The very first attempt at this yielded the game _Kingseat_. Because it was the first attempt, it wasn't ideal. It became too complicated to actually play without table/while waiting in line. It had a few weak spots. But that's why we make a first attempt: to learn from it and create something better!

A year or so later, I came back to the project as a wiser game designer (I like to believe) and fixed it. Kingseat still couldn't be played in line, but at least it was a much stronger and more polished game. Instead, that single idea turned into **4 games**: _Smallseat, Queenseat, Kingseat, Kaizerseat_.

The first two can be played while standing up. The first one has more cute animals and simpler rules, so it might also be played with kids. Kaizerseat, on the other hand, makes the idea _more_ involved and turns it into a full-fledged board game.

With these 4 games, I could explore the core idea in every possible direction, and create an experience for everyone. It was one of the first times I created a "collection" of tightly related games.

_What was that core idea?_

* A game where one position---the throne---is most important. They have slight powers (first in order, preference in ties, maybe literal special powers).
* Which means that half your actions are about _changing places_. (Hence why it can be played without table, and it's quite an "active game")
* And the other half are about making sure your _preferred (political) party gets the most votes_. 

That final point is most important here. For each of these games, I had to invent 12 made-up animal factions. Not only did I need a varied (and hopefully balanced) set of special powers, I also needed icons ("coat of arms"), special abilities, backstory, a cool slogan/motto for their faction, etcetera.

### Game to Story
As I wrote all of these, I noticed I was basically "worldbuilding" for a story. I could easily see a short story, perhaps aimed at children or more "fairy tale"-like, where all these factions fight over that throne. I started referencing other factions in backstories, and general "long-term goals" that a faction might try to achieve.

And so I wrote down something like ...

> "Create a short story for each game? Might be fun. I can reuse the work I already did. And it's an easy way to get a story about politics/power to appeal to younger readers." 

I never intended this to be like a "main release" from me. I would just write these books, publish them without much fanfare, and that's that. A little side project to _experiment_ with new writing methods, to get a break from my larger "main" work.

A few days later, I turned this into a more specific plan.

* Each book would contain 1 chapter per faction. The entire thing is told from _their_ point of view.
* This means 12 chapters. If I make each 1,500-3,500 words, I can publish one book per game at a reasonable length. 
	* (Still comparatively short, but at least above 20,000 words. Below that point, the books are just too thin and people don't think they're worth buying, is my experience.)
* Each one uses the unique rules from that game to show how a different "political system" (or rather "system of power") might work, pros and cons, how it could be manipulated (or not).
	* The one constant through them is of course that **throne**: a mythical moving throne that shows up in random places. But if you own it/sit on it, then you get some important power.
	* Which means all these stories are about voting, swapping places, and trying to get your faction to win in the end.

For example, I imagined the first book (aimed at the youngest audience) to start with someone who is bossed around by their parents, discovers the throne, and is like "I _need_ to have it so I can boss my parents around!" 

Every faction has their own reason for wanting the throne, and their own way of trying to get it, and that's how the story progresses until _someone_ wins in the end. (Though maybe not. It's a _magically moving_ throne, after all.)

I thought this plan was _fine_. But I missed something really different or interesting about it. I decided to leave it for now. I know that, over time, my brain will suddenly realize something once in a while, until a project is "good enough to start with".

### The final ideas
In this case, I realized I wanted to practice more with _narration_. The old adage of "show don't tell" is crucial, but also easier said than done. Similarly, people usually rank a really distinctive and fun narration/writing style _higher_ (when it comes to what makes them enjoy a book) than the actual content of the book.

And so I made up the final "guiding principle" for these books.

> The books are (almost) entirely TELL DON'T SHOW. The narrator is incredibly present, most things are told in a fun way instead of shown happening, and the stories are portrayed like someone telling folklore around a campfire.

For the first book I wanted to make most sentences _rhyme_ too, but that fades over time as the story grows. (Let's pretend it's a metaphor for how the corruption of power ruins innocence or something. And not just because it's exhausting for both me and the reader if a book rhymes for 100 pages.)

I _also_ wanted to do this to practice with a highly unreliable narrator. Time and time again, there are subtle differences in how the narrator tells something, and then how a character speaks about (the "truth") later. This fits the theme of power and politics even more.

Now _why_ would they be unreliable? I always think you need a very strong reason for it.

> The **narrator** turns out to be the one who _created_ the throne and is chasing it down. It started as an attempt at better politics/systems of rule, went wrong, and now they're trying to fix it without giving away _they_ made the mistake.

With these guidelines set, I thought I had enough for some solid stories. Even if only to _challenge myself_ to try new writing styles and approaches.

## Smallseat
If you've followed me for a while, you know I don't really plan, or make outlines, or write huge documents about my magical worlds. I'm an improviser. I'd just waste time creating all those extra plans, while being so overwhelmed by 20 pages about my plot/world/characters that I never ever actually read it back.

Instead, I keep a _short_ list called "metadata" which answers the most important core/common questions. For this one,

* **Start =** a throne suddenly lands in a cozy fairy tale forest. With it comes a mysterious person too (the "Teller"). The forest is _great_ (peace, prosperity, etcetera). We start with that kid who is bossed around by their parents.
* **Goal =** The Teller gives the deadline and goal. They must _vote_ (by giving him some magical piece of wood with the sigil of their preferred faction). Winner gets this magical throne with all its power.
* **Swapping Rule =** You can only vote for a faction if you've lived a day in their life. (If you've "swapped places" with them.)

The "Start" is supposed to give me a strong first chapter to kick things off. The "Goal" is the general thread that gives momentum for the entire story. The "Swapping Rule" describes a crucial part of the "political system" (or "how the throne works") in this book.

Anything else? We'll figure that out as we go. At least I already have all the factions and their backstories/goals to go on!
